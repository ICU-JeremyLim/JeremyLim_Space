## 1. Introduction

Southern Min (閩南語), also known as Hokkien, has lasted an impactful influence on the lexicons of several Southeast Asian languages through centuries of Chinese merchant migration, such as the huge amount of migration during the period of late Qing dynasty and Republican periods, known as Xia NanYang (下南洋) in Chinese context. In the Philippines, this influence is most directly documented in Tagalog, where Chan-Yap (1977) identified 163 Hokkien loanwords covering the semantic ranges of kinship, Southern Min food, commerce, and domestic life. What is striking is that a bunch of words that represent the uniqueness of Southern Min culture were adopted to Tagalog and Cebuano. For example, [ín-chek] (Southern Min for “his uncle”) became [inˈtsik] in many Philippine languages, including Cebuano; this likely reflects intermarriage between Southern Min merchants and local women, which carried kinship terms into the local languages alongside commercial ones. Another example is that the jargon used for gambling in Southern Min were used in Philippine languages as well, such as [pak̚⁴.kiau⁵³] (Southern Min) and [pak̚ˈkjaw] (Cebuano). Furthermore, the expression of “to murder” in Southern Min is ‘pasilang’, literally meaning to fight someone till the person dies, is also frequently used in the Philippines, such as in official news. However, the phonological mapping rules of Hokkien-derived loanwords in Philippine languages has received comparatively little systematic analysis. This paper, grateful to the help of a Cebuano speaker, via an experimental phonology way, focus in the investigation of Phonological Adaptation of Southern Min Loanwords in Cebuano, the most widely spoken Austronesian language in the Philippines, with over 20 million speakers concentrated in the Visayas and Mindanao regions (Shryock 1993: p. 103).

This paper documents and analyzes the phonological adaptation of Hokkien loanwords into Cebuano, drawing on a corpus of 65 lexical items based on the wordlist of Hokkien loanwords in Tagalog (Chan-Yap 1977) and elicited from CEB001, a native Cebuano speaker. The reason for using the wordlist documented by Chan-Yap is because there are many common Southern Min loanwords in Tagalog and Cebuano, which lays a solid foundation for this case study. As a result, fourteen major phonological rules are identified and formalized, covering consonant adaptation (nasalization loss, nasal place assimilation, aspiration, /ts/ replacement, glottal stop alternations, coda stop preservation), vowel adaptation (ɔ→o substitution, glide formation, diphthong restructuring, vowel raising, and lowering), and morphemelevel processes (truncation, epenthesis, and reduplication).

This research further argues that certain phonological phenomena, such as the observed variation in aspiration and affricate replacement, is best ex- plained by positing two historical routes of borrowing: (a) direct Southern Min-to-Cebuano borrowing, and (b) indirect borrowing via Tagalog. Meanwhile, this hypothesis accounts for several otherwise puzzling exceptions in the data. The structure of the paper is as follows: Section 2 describes the data collection methodology; Sections 3–5 constitute the core results and discussion, organized by phonological domain; Section 6 provides a synthesis and rule summary; Section 7 concludes with directions for future research; and the full lexical corpus is provided in Appendix A, following the Works Cited and AI Usage Report.

## 2. Methodology

Data were collected through structured elicitation sessions with CEB001, a native speaker of Cebuano. The elicitation proceeded in three stages.

First, a wordlist of 65 candidate Hokkien-origin lexical items was compiled by cross-referencing the Tagalog loanword inventory in Chan-Yap (1977) with standard Cebuano dictionaries, notably Wolff (1972) and Bunye & Yap (1971). There are certain words that do not exist in dictionaries, relying on the native check by CEB001. The Hokkien source forms were verified against the Ministry of Education Taiwanese Hokkien online dictionary (sutian.moe.edu.tw) and the Revised Formosan Pronunciation Dictionary (chhoe.taigi.info), both of which provide standardized IPA transcriptions with tone notation for citation forms.

Second, NAN001 (the author of this paper), a native Southern Min speaker, served as a secondary consultant to confirm that the proposed source forms fell within the productive lexicon of Southern Min. The validity of using Taiwanese Southern Min dictionaries is because (a) which is the only available and accessible systematically organized dictionary; (b) Taiwanese Southern Min represents the mixture of Quanzhou and Zhangzhou accents, resembling the accents of migration to the Philippines.

Third, CEB001 was asked to produce each lexical item in citation form, and recordings were made. Phonetic transcriptions were produced by the researcher using IPA notation based on close listening, with particular attention to the realization of coda stops, aspiration contrasts, stress placement, and vowel quality. Cebuano IPA forms were cross-checked against Binisaya.com and Wiktionary where available. The transcriptions represent CEB001’s production and may not generalize to all Cebuano dialects or speakers.

The resulting corpus was analyzed by formulating phonological rules in a descriptive format, grouping them into three domains: consonant-relevant rules, vowel-relevant rules, and morpheme-level rules. Where a rule has counterexamples in the data, these are flagged explicitly. Tagalog comparanda are drawn from Chan-Yap (1977) and serve a comparative rather than primary analytical role in this research. The full corpus is presented in Appendix A; in-text examples throughout Sections 3–5 are drawn from and consistent with that corpus.

## 3. Results and Discussion: Consonant-Relevant Rules

### 3.1. Nasalization Loss

In Southern Min (Hokkien), nasalized vowels (Ṽ) contrast phonemically with their oral counterparts and can form minimal pairs. For example, [suã²¹] ‘mountain’ is distinct from [sua²¹] ‘sand’. Cebuano, by contrast, lacks phonemic vowel nasality: nasalized vowels do not exist in the Cebuano phoneme inventory and therefore do not form minimal pairs. Consequently, Southern Min loanwords consistently undergo nasalization loss in adaptation.

Across all examples [1.(1)–(7)], nasality is categorically dropped. Aryanti (2021: p. 255) documents the same process in Hokkien-to-Indonesian loanword adaptation, noting that vowel nasality is not contrastive in Indonesian and is therefore lost. The same filter applies in Cebuano.

### 3.2. Nasal Place Assimilation

Southern Min disyllabic words are composed of two morphologically independent Chinese characters, and their internal syllable boundaries are perceptually salient to speakers of Sinitic languages. In Cebuano, however, these words are borrowed as monomorphemic units, with the original morpheme boundary reanalyzed as a simple intervocalic consonant cluster. This reanalysis accelerates assimilation at the first-to-second syllable boundary, since speakers are no longer required to maintain the segmental identity of each constituent morpheme.

Four nasal place assimilation rules are attested. The general environment is: in a disyllabic word of the shape (C)VC.CV(C), when the nasal coda of the first syllable is followed by a stop onset of a different place of articulation, the nasal assimilates to the place of the following stop.

This process is typologically well-attested in loanword phonology and has been described as a form of regressive place assimilation conditioned by the following obstruent (cf. Aryanti 2021: p. 256; Kang & Katsuda 2024). Crucially, as example 2.(4) demonstrates, assimilation does not apply when the following segment is not a stop, confirming that the environment is strictly restricted to [+stop] onsets.

### 3.3. Aspiration and Deaspiration

Aspirated stops [pʰ, tʰ, kʰ, tsʰ] contrast phonemically with their unaspirated counterparts in Southern Min. In Tagalog, aspiration is not phonemic, and all Hokkien aspirated stops are deaspirated categorically upon borrowing (Chan-Yap 1977: pp. 18–19). Cebuano presents a more complex picture: aspiration is partially phonemic, with minimal pairs documented for /t/ vs. /tʰ/ and /p/ vs. /pʰ/ (CEB001, personal communication), while /kʰ/ is absent from the Cebuano consonant inventory.

4 This asymmetry produces three distinct outcomes: (a) /kʰ/ is categorically deaspirated (Rule 3.1) because Cebuano lacks /kʰ/ entirely; (b) /tʰ/ is preserved where it contrasts (Rule 3.2); and (c) the behavior of /tsʰ/ and /pʰ/ is variable, conditioned by the historical borrowing path. The hypothesis that two borrowing routes coexist, one directly from Southern Min, one via Tagalog, was based on the lexical overlapping of Southern Min loanwords in Tagalog and Cebuano, and the present data suggest that the dual-route model is necessary for Cebuano. In other words, without this dual-route model, the phonological explanation is insufficient to cover the variations of loanwords’ pronunciation affected by historical and lexical interactions.

### 3.4. /ts/ Replacement

The affricate /ts/ is absent from the native Cebuano consonant inventory. Southern Min /ts/ is therefore subject to substitution upon borrowing, with the specific output segment conditioned by the borrowing route and phonological environment. Four replacement patterns are attested:

Rule 4.1: ts, tsʰ →s [Tagalog-route] Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (1) [tsu⁵³.kʰeʔ³²] [suˈki] (2) [gin²⁴.tsam⁵³] [ginˈsam] (3) [tsʰĩ⁵⁵.tau³³] [siˈtaw] Rule 4.2: ts →ʃ [Tagalog-route; environment: _i+V] Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (4) [tsui⁵³.kui⁵³] [ʃoˈkoy] Rule 4.3: ts →t [Tagalog-route] Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (5) [a⁵⁵.tsi⁵³] [ˈa.te] Rule 4.4: ts →tsʰ [direct Southern Min route] Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (6) [baʔ³².tsui⁵³] [bat̚ˈtsʰoj] (7) [dzi³³.tsi⁵³] [diˈtsʰe] The contrast between Rule 4.3 and Rule 4.4 provides compelling evidence for the dual-route hypothesis. Both [ˈa.te] ‘elder sister’ and [diˈtsʰe] ‘second elder sister’ derive from Southern Min /tsi/ and share the meaning ‘sister’, yet they surface with different consonants in Cebuano. If all Southern Min loanwords entered Cebuano via a single path, words with identical source segments and similar meanings would be expected to surface identically. The divergence strongly suggests that [ˈa.te] passed through Tagalog (where /ts/ →/t/), while [diˈtsʰe] was borrowed directly from Southern Min (where /ts/ was adapted as aspirated /tsʰ/, a segment that exists in Cebuano).

It is noteworthy that Rule 3.3 and Rule 4.4 together indicate an asymmetry in the Cebuano consonant inventory itself, not just in the borrowing 6 path: the plain affricate /ts/ does not exist in Cebuano, but its aspirated counterpart /tsʰ/ does. This is why direct Southern Min borrowings preserve /ts/ as /tsʰ/ (Rule 4.4) rather than as /ts/, while Tagalog-route borrowings, which lack aspiration altogether, resolve /ts/ to /s/ or /t/ instead (Rules 4.1, 4.3).

### 3.5. Glottal Stop Deletion and Insertion

Southern Min checked-tone syllables bear a final glottal stop /ʔ/ which is deleted in Cebuano. There is only one environment for this deletion: the glottal stop is removed whenever it occupies the coda of a syllable, regardless of whether that syllable is the first or the second syllable of the word. The deletion is categorical across the corpus. A superficially paradoxical insertion rule also exists: glottal stop is inserted between two adjacent vowels (/V_V/) because hiatus (a sequence of two tautosyllabic vowels) is disallowed in Cebuano phonotactics.

Rule 5.1: ʔ →∅/ _]σ (coda deletion) Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (1) [pʰaʔ³².si⁵³.laŋ²⁴] [pasˈlaŋ] (2) [peʔ⁴.tsʰai²¹] [pʰeˈtsʰay] (3) [baʔ³².mi³³] [ˈma.mi] (4) [hoŋ³³.baʔ³²] [humˈba] (5) [hiaʔ⁴] [hiˈja] (6) [bak̚⁴.kʰiaʔ⁴] [bak̚ˈkia] Rule 5.2*: ∅→ʔ / V_V (hiatus repair) Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (7) [bi⁵³.lau³³] [biˈlaʔ.o] Rule 5.2 is marked with an asterisk because it is not universally applicable in the corpus: only a single example of vowel-hiatus repair was found, so the rule is best treated as conditional rather than fully general pending further data.

The contrast between glottal deletion and glottal insertion is not paradoxical but complementary: glottal stop is removed when it would produce an unacceptable coda environment within the Southern Min checked syllable, and inserted when vowel hiatus would otherwise arise.

### 3.6. Coda Stop Preservation and Insertion

One of the most typologically salient features of CEB001’s Cebuano loanword phonology is the preservation of Hokkien checked-tone coda stops (/k̚/, /t̚/) as unreleased stops in the Cebuano output. This stands in sharp contrast to Tagalog, where the same coda stops are deleted (Chan-Yap 1977: pp. 19–20).

## 4. Results and Discussion: Vowel-Relevant Rules

### 4.1. ɔ →o Substitution

Southern Min /ɔ/ is a low-mid back rounded vowel. Cebuano’s vowel inventory comprises five vowels: /i, e, a, o, u/. The Southern Min /ɔ/ is mapped to Cebuano /o/, the closest native approximation, across the corpus.

The /ɔ/→/o/ mapping is also attested in Indonesian Hokkien loanwords, where /ɔ/ is similarly absent from the target vowel inventory (Aryanti 2021: p. 258).

### 4.5. Association of Cebuano Stress Pattern and Vowel Position

According to CEB001, high vowels /i, u/ occur far more frequently than mid vowels /e, o/, suggesting that high vowels are the marked category in Cebuano, while /e/ and /o/ are unmarked. This markedness split also has a historical basis: the native Cebuano vowel system consists only of /i, a, u/, with /e/ and /o/ entering the inventory later, primarily through loanwords (Bunye & Yap 1971; Wolff 1972). The data discussed in Sections 4.3 and 4.4 do not show a simple one-to-one association between vowel markedness and stress, since the unstressed position permits both high and mid vowels (§4.4): a stressed syllable requires a marked high vowel, but an unstressed syllable is not restricted to the unmarked mid vowel. This pattern is visualized in Figure 1, which illustrates the connection between vowel markedness and stress position; the precise mechanism underlying this association, however, remains a question for further research.

Furthermore, the selection of which syllable is stressed is itself unpredictable from the Southern Min source. Shryock’s (1993) research shows that stress preferentially settles on the penultimate syllable when that syllable is heavy, which partly predicts the Cebuano stress pattern; however, this prediction cannot be derived from Southern Min syllable structure alone, because multiple phonological changes apply simultaneously during borrowing, and the Southern Min input attested in present-day dictionaries may differ from the historical input that actually entered Cebuano. For instance, [ke⁵⁵.ti³³] in example 10.(7) has a light penultimate syllable, a case for which Shryock’s theory does not predict stress placement one way or the other; yet the word surfaces in Cebuano as [ˈki.tit], with penultimate stress and raising. How this particular outcome arose historically cannot be recovered from the present data, and is a further instance of the unpredictability noted throughout this section.

## 5. Results and Discussion: Morpheme-Level Rules

### 5.1. Morpheme Deletion (Trisyllabic Truncation)

Southern Min trisyllabic words, typically compound morphemes of three Chinese characters, tend to be reduced upon borrowing into Cebuano, likely because Cebuano has a strong preference for disyllabic lexical words. Four examples are attested, displaying different truncation strategies:

Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (1) [ka³³.i⁵⁵.tʰai²⁴] [kaˈtay] (2) [liam²⁴.tɔ³³.baʔ³²] [ljemˈpo] (3) [pʰaʔ³².si⁵³.laŋ²⁴] [pasˈlaŋ] (4) [kio²¹.kio²¹.kʰau²¹] [kjawˈkjaw] Example 12.(1) shows deletion of the penultimate syllable; example 12.(2) shows deletion of the final syllable. In both cases, the initial syllable is preserved, suggesting a preference for retaining word-initial material. Example 12.(3) involves a more complex process: the vowel of the penultimate 13 syllable is deleted, and its onset migrates to the coda position of the preceding syllable (syllable fusion). Example 12.(4) involves syllable fusion (/kio/ + /kʰau/ →/kiau/ →/kjaw/) followed by reduplication, mirroring the disyllabic reduplicative pattern of the Southern Min source.

The process appears sensitive to the specific syllabic structure and segmental content of the source word, and may also reflect morpheme-level transparency and historical variations. Thus, no consistent deletion rule can be stated.

### 5.2. Epenthesis (Syllabic Nasal Resolution)

Southern Min has syllabic nasals /sŋ̩/ and /tŋ̩/ that form complete syllable without a vowel. Cebuano does not permit syllabic consonants: all syllables must have a vowel. A vowel is therefore inserted to break up the syllabic nasal and produce a well-formed CVC syllable.

Rule 13.1: ∅→V / C_ŋ̩ Southern Min (Hokkien) Cebuano (1) [laŋ²⁴.sŋ̩²⁴] [lanˈsoŋ] (2) [hue⁵⁵.tŋ̩²⁴] [huweˈteŋ] The selection of the inserted vowel is not yet fully predictable: the closest acoustic approximation of the syllabic nasal /ŋ̩/ is /eŋ/; however, /sŋ̩/ → /soŋ/ in 13.(1) and /tŋ̩/ →/teŋ/ in 13.(2) yield /oŋ/ and /eŋ/ respectively. This variation requires additional data to firm generalization.

### 5.3. Reduplication

Two items in the corpus surface in Cebuano as reduplicated forms: [kjawˈkjaw] (already introduced as example 12.(4)) and [ˈbi.lo-ˈbi.lo].

The derivation of [kjawˈkjaw] is discussed in §5.1: the Southern Min source [kio²¹.kio²¹.kʰau²¹] first undergoes syllable fusion of its second and third syllables (/kio/ + /kʰau/ →/kiau/ →/kjaw/), and only the resulting disyllabic form [kjaw] is reduplicated. The likely motivation for the reduplication step itself is that the Southern Min source word already has reduplicated material in its first two syllables ([kio²¹.kio²¹...]), so the Cebuano output preserves that reduplicative shape even though the specific syllable being copied has changed.

The derivation of [ˈbi.lo-ˈbi.lo] ‘bilu-bilo’ is different. Its Southern Min source is the single (non-reduplicated) form [bi⁵³.lut̚⁴]. To arrive at [ˈbi.lo], this source must have undergone checked-tone coda deletion (/t̚/ →∅, unlike the general pattern of checked-tone coda preservation discussed in §3.6) and vowel lowering (/u/ →/o/, parallel to Rule 11.1), before the resulting disyllabic form was reduplicated to produce [ˈbi.lo-ˈbi.lo]. The deletion of the checked-tone coda here, despite its general preservation elsewhere in the corpus, may be conditioned by the reduplication itself, or may instead reflect the dual-route hypothesis (§3.3): the Tagalog form of this word is 14 bilu-bilo, which has no checked coda to begin with, so if this item entered Cebuano via Tagalog rather than directly from Southern Min, only vowel lowering, and not coda deletion, would need to be posited.

Taken together, both reduplicated items suggest the same general pattern: the source word first undergoes one or more ordinary phonological changes, and only the resulting form is reduplicated afterward. This ordering is not yet established as a general rule, however, and further investigation with additional reduplicated items is needed to confirm it.

## 7. Conclusion

This paper has documented fourteen phonological rules governing the adaptation of Southern Min loanwords into Cebuano, based on fieldwork data from consultant CEB001. The rules span three domains: consonant adaptation, vowel adaptation, and morpheme-level processes.

Several empirical findings are noteworthy. First, Cebuano systematically preserves Hokkien checked-tone coda stops (/k̚/, /t̚/) where Tagalog categorically deletes them, with the Tagalog coda stop itself undergoing deletion in the same syllable-coda position where the Southern Min source originally had a checked tone. This asymmetric mapping is an instance of what Hsieh et al. (2009) call transphonologization, where a contrast from the source language survives in the borrowing language but is re-encoded onto a different native structure; here, Cebuano keeps the checked-tone codas as unreleased stops, while Tagalog does not. Second, the observed variation in aspiration and affricate replacement is explained by a dual borrowing-route hypothesis: items that entered Cebuano directly from Southern Min preserve aspiration and aspirated affricates, while items that entered via Tagalog lost aspiration and underwent /ts/ simplification. Third, vowel raising and lowering are conditioned by stress position, a finding that connects the loanword phonology to the native Cebuano stress system described in Shryock (1993), and which further suggests an asymmetric licensing relationship between stress and vowel markedness (§4.5).

Several questions remain open. The counterexample to the vowel raising rule, and the related cases in which an unstressed syllable retains a high vowel instead of lowering (§4.4), are not yet fully explained; stress position is a strong predictor overall, but the present dataset cannot rule 16 out an additional conditioning factor. The epenthetic vowel selection in syllabic nasal resolution (Rule 13.1) also lacks a principled explanation. The absence of /p̚/ coda stops in the corpus may reflect a real phonotactic gap or may be a result of the limited sample. Future work should include additional elicitation sessions with CEB001, acceptability judgment tasks using artificial words, and data from additional Cebuano and Tagalog speakers and dialects to assess the generalizability of the present findings.

More broadly, this study contributes to a growing body of work on Southeast Asian Hokkien loanword phonology, joining comparable analyses of Indonesian (Aryanti 2021) and Tagalog (Chan-Yap 1977). The comparison of Cebuano with both Indonesian and Tagalog reveals that the phonological adaptation of Hokkien loanwords is language-specific even within geographically and historically related contact communities. What is preserved in one language may be deleted in another, and the specific adaptation strategies reflect the unique phonological grammar of each receiving language, as mathematical functions with conditional constraints, rather than a universal adaptation template.
