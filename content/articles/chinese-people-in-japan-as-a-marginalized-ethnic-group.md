According to Yamashiro, there are two kinds of people from the point of view of Japanese society: Japanese and non-Japanese (175). Non-Japanese are minorities, marginalized and regarded as inferior groups in Japanese society (Yamashiro 175). Furthermore, the non-Japanese group has a hierarchical structure from the mainstream Japanese people’s perspective (Zhang 211). The most acceptable groups for mainstream Japanese ethnic groups are European or American; the second level is Southeast Asian or Korean; finally, the most unacceptable group is Chinese because of the fluctuating political relationship, unresolved socio-historical issues, and cultural differences (Zhang 211). This essay will analyze from a chronological approach why Chinese people are marginalized and discriminated against in Japanese society. This marginalization and discrimination have a deep historical root, continue in current Japanese society, and will be more challenging in the future.

  

Throughout contemporary history, Chinese people in Japan have been a marginalized group, often occupying a low socio-economic status because of imperialist ideology, societal bias, and the socio-economic gap between China and Japan. In 1859, the opening of the port of Yokohama attracted many Western mechanics unfamiliar with dealing with Eastern countries (Ito). To promote their business, they hired many Chinese people from Southern China to help them communicate with the Japanese by writing down Chinese characters and serving their daily needs, such as providing food and clothes. However, Chinese workers were often treated with little respect and social recognition (Ito). In 1895, the Qing dynasty lost the First Sino-Japanese War, resulting in two waves of Chinese people immigrating to Japan simultaneously (Tsu 208). The first wave was the annexation of Taiwan by Japan, which led many Taiwanese people to automatically obtain Japanese nationality and immigrate to Japan as laborers. The second wave was when the Qing dynasty reflected on its loss and started to admire the achievements of the Meiji Restoration, so the government of the Qing sent many Chinese students to study abroad in Japan. Both Taiwanese and Qing students were regarded as second-class residents in Japan during that period, where Japan and the Qing dynasty had a significant socioeconomic gap (Tsu 209). In the Showa era, the rise of extreme nationalism and imperialism resulted in direct discrimination toward Chinese people and the use of a stigmatized word, “Shina” for Chinese people. After WWII, till the 1980s, China and Japan reestablished normal diplomatic relationships, and many Chinese people immigrated to Japan due to the economic boom during the post-Showa and Heisei era (Chen 5). Nevertheless, in order to control the number of Chinese immigrants, the Japanese government restricted the available occupations of Chinese people, such as chief, tailor, and barber; all of them were at the bottom of society and discriminated against by Japanese society as “Gaijin” (Chen 10). This long history of exclusion continues to shape how Chinese people are perceived in Japan today.

  

Currently, there are approximately 784 thousand Chinese immigrants in Japan, sharing 31 percent of the total number of immigrants in Japan, consisting of workers, specialists, students, and spouses or children of permanent residents (Buchholz). Despite their large population and diverse roles, Chinese immigrants continue to experience discrimination and marginalization due to the prejudice of Japanese society. A typical case is that Chinese workers or specialists in Japan tend to be regarded as competitors to Japanese people in the job market (Zhang 206). Particularly, the decline of the Chinese economy in recent years has made many Chinese people, who cannot find a suitable job in China, rush to Japan, which is geographically close to their hometowns, culturally similar to China, and financially more affordable than Western countries. This type of immigration is called “Runri” on the internet, resulting in fostering Japanese people’s anti-Chinese-immigrants attitudes because they believe that the advent of Chinese people will result in the rise of housing prices, the increase of the crime rate, and the disharmony of the local community (Turner). As a result of the anti-Chinese sentiment, Chinese students in Japan also suffer various types of discrimination. The first example is that Chinese students commonly face challenging situations when renting a house in Japan because Japanese house holders stereotype them as messy and troublesome (Zhang 202). Another instance is the Japanese language schools, based on my experiences, which are institutions with a strong bias towards Chinese students: they strictly count their attendance and use visa control as a threat because they assume that Chinese students’ purpose of enrolling in Japanese language schools is not to study but to gain residence for illegal work. Once Chinese students’ attendance rates are lower than 80 percent, the Japanese language school will immediately report the case to the Japanese government, resulting in the unfair cancellation of the student visa. Additionally, the increasing number of Chinese travelers to Japan strengthened the stereotype in the Japanese mind of “Chinese way of doing things”: some of them speak loudly in public places, do not line up at the bus station, and throw garbage everywhere (Zhang 210). Unfortunately, a few Chinese travelers’ “non-Japaneseness” behaviors exacerbate Japanese citizens’ negative impression of the majority of Chinese people, further strengthening the discrimination towards Chinese immigrants in Japan.

  

It is predictable from the present challenges, combined with the hostile media environment and tense political situation, that the Chinese people in Japan will be further marginalized or discriminated against in the near future. Although China and Japan experienced a brief period of “Honeymoon” after China’s “Reform and opening up” policy, the turning point that worsened the China-Japan relationship was the Diaoyu Island dispute in 2012 (Drifte 1). Since that period, Chinese media have produced a tremendous amount of anti-Japan television dramas and articles, which have circulated widely in society (Lague and Lee). Similarly, the Japanese side frequently disseminates negative portrayals of China through mainstream media. Such a hostile media environment has resulted in a tense bilateral relationship, disrupted various forms of China-Japan communication at both the political and societal levels, and exacerbated negative public images that the two societies hold for each other. A statistical survey conducted in 2024 reveals this worsening bilateral relationship: approximately 90 percent of Japanese people possess a negative image of Chinese people, and vice versa (The Genron NPO). The second central turning point is the worsening US-China relationship, accompanied by the anti-globalism tendency after the pandemic, indirectly resulting in the rapid rise of right-wing political parties in Japan and the sense of estrangement between China and Japan. Noticeably, this year, the fringe right wings, including the Sansei Party and Nippon Ishin no Kai, reached 15% in the national election of the Upper House of Japan collectively  (Kelly and Geddie). Inspired by the nationalist values of Trump, they advocate “Japanese first” and propose new policies aimed at tightening immigration policy and restricting foreigners’ rights in Japan, particularly toward Chinese people who are “silently invading Japan” in their view (Kelly and Geddie). Therefore, the negative view and disadvantageous policies in Japan towards Chinese immigrants foreshadow a more serious discriminatory atmosphere in the future.

  

In conclusion, the case of Chinese people in Japan shows a long trajectory of marginalization. The historical developments reveal that Chinese immigrants in Japan have been consistently positioned at the margin of Japanese society, formulating a structural pattern of discrimination that is still influential in the current situation and the future. Nowadays, despite the increasing arrival of Chinese people, they continue to be perceived as outsiders regardless of their status as workers or students, suffering discrimination in their lives. Looking ahead, the worsening China-Japan relationship and the rise of nationalist politics in Japan suggest that these challenges will intensify in this era. This analysis of Chinese people in Japan aligns with the point of view of Yamashiro that Japanese society constructs a rigid boundary between Japanese and non-Japanese, indicating that this structural discrimination in Japan is historically rooted and extends across various minority groups. This issue requires deliberate efforts to address in the future, such as strengthening the education on peace and reconciliation, promoting cross-cultural exchange in Japanese society, and improving diplomatic relationships between Japan and China, so that Japanese society can be more inclusive toward Chinese people while contributing to the well-being of the two nations.
