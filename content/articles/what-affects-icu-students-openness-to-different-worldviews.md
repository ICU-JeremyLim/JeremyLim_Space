Abstract

This Research paper used an interview survey approach combined with descriptive statistics to investigate how language diversity and cognitive flexibility among ICU students affect their openness to different worldviews, attending a Japanese Liberal Arts Top Global University Project (Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, Japan (MEXT), n.d.). The results showed that 100% (6/6) of the sample participants were interested in exploring different worldviews and had an overall high average openness score (7.7/10). Furthermore, a robust positive correlation (β = 0.434, r = 0.77)  between language diversity and openness, and a slightly positive correlation (β = 0.412, r = 0.44) between cognitive flexibility and openness, were revealed. The result indicated that using second languages, cultivating critical thinking skills, and interacting with people from multicultural backgrounds may contribute to individuals’ high openness to different worldviews. Thus, educational institutions can take these effects into consideration to cultivate students’ openness, thereby contributing to a harmonious society in the future. [149 words]

Keywords: Openness to Different Worldviews, Language Diversity, Cognitive Flexibility, ICU Students 

  

Introduction

As the trend of globalization has accelerated over the past several decades, a high level of openness to diverse worldviews has become a key element in constructing a harmonious and inclusive society, where people’s co-workers, neighbors, and friends come from diverse backgrounds with different languages, cultures, and identities. Without openness and tolerance for diverse ideas, society may be filled with misunderstanding, hatred, and conflict, jeopardizing both overall productivity and the general well-being of citizens. On this foundation, it is meaningful to examine the level of openness and its causes among university students, as they represent the future of society and offer a predictive perspective. Admittedly, previous research clearly shows that the college experience generally increases American students’ openness through multicultural engagement, as evidenced by statistical data (Pascarella, 1996). However, the effects of other factors, such as language diversity and cognitive flexibility, on openness to different worldviews have not yet been systematically examined by any known researcher. 

To address this research gap, this paper will focus on International Christian University (ICU) students, as its highly interactive, multicultural, and multilingual environment offers a unique and ideal context for examining the relationships among language diversity, cognitive flexibility, and openness. Specifically, according to the ICU religious center, 20% of ICU senior students at the time of graduation are Christians, which is double the 10% proportion in their freshman year. This change in the Christian community indicates that a significant proportion of students experience a worldview shift during their university years. Nevertheless, this trend is not taken for granted; it reveals an unusual pattern of high openness to the Christian faith, or, more generally, a different worldview among ICU students, since Japanese society is basically conservative and the proportion of Christians in Japan is approximately 1.6% at the point of 2010 (Pew Research Center, 2012). Therefore, the high openness of ICU students may be shaped by the multicultural atmosphere, diverse language environment, and liberal arts education in the unique ICU community. As such, the research question (purpose) for this project is: 

  

RQ: How do language diversity and cognitive flexibility co-affect ICU students' openness to different worldviews?

  

One of the goals of this research project was to consider the topic of ICU students’ openness to different worldviews from an interdisciplinary perspective to provide a broader view that may include or extend beyond the current research purpose and context. Consequently, the following three disciplines in the Literature Review below were selected based on the author’s own interest in the discipline and the topic. 

  

Interdisciplinary Literature Review of Openness to Different Worldviews

Religious Studies and Worldviews

In East Asian society, differences in worldviews are frequently perceived as the source of potential conflict. That is why people usually do not publicly discuss their faith to maintain social harmony. However, for ICU students, how can openness to different worldviews be shaped at multiple levels in a multi-worldview environment that differs from East Asian society? In particular, ICU students are actively involved in exploring diverse worldviews, such as taking an introduction to Christianity course, engaging in dialogue on controversial topics, and even being roommates with someone who practices a different faith.  Thus, this fact indicates that the traditional East Asian view of worldview is insufficient to analyze the openness of ICU students to different worldviews. Corresponding to this pattern, theological scholars suggest that worldview is not a narrow view of it as a faith-based or belief-based disagreement, but rather comprises three dimensions: propositional beliefs, behavioral practices, and an underlying heart orientation (Schultz, 2013). In other words, a worldview is something that people live out rather than agree with abstract ideas without a corresponding lifestyle. Therefore, this three-dimensional framework provides a perspective to explore how ICU students’ openness to different worldviews is constructed through interaction with people from diverse cultural, linguistic, and religious backgrounds. Language Education and Worldviews

Learning a new language requires learners to adopt a new way of thinking and consciously reconstruct their cultural identity and worldview. For instance, people who learn Japanese as a second language have to acquire honorific words, which involves understanding and adapting to the traditional Japanese worldviews based on strict societal hierarchy and the order of seniority. Likewise, ICU students frequently learn, use, and practice different languages in their lives. In this way, it is meaningful to carefully examine whether linguistic diversity enables ICU students to become more open to different worldviews. According to a previous study, language functions as a carrier of worldviews and cultural heritage; particularly, this carrier plays an active role in shaping individuals’ worldviews when they use different languages in intercultural interactions (Ke, 2010). Given this theory, ICU students' language diversity, shaped by consistent language learning through ELA or JLP projects and active interactions with people who speak different languages in bilingual-based campus life, such as preparing the ICU festival, joining dormitory events, as well as participating in clubs, will likely lead to a general openness to diverse worldviews. Cognitive Psychology and Worldviews

In the psychology field, cognitive flexibility usually refers to the ability to adjust thinking patterns when encountering disagreement, uncertainty, or difficulty, which is highly related to how individuals respond to new worldviews or different perspectives. As for ICU students, this ability may be particularly outstanding because ICU’s liberal arts education encourages critical thinking and interdisciplinary learning, which require students to think outside the box and continuously revise their assumptions to adapt to the unpredictable society in the future. Additionally, the ICU entrance exam offers diverse pathways rather than a single standardized test. This model selects students who value problem-solving skills and flexible adaptability.  In this sense, extraordinary cognitive flexibility among ICU students may facilitate their high openness to diverse worldviews. Echoing this hypothesis, a questionnaire-based cognitive psychology experiment found a moderate positive correlation among the Cognitive Flexibility Questionnaire, creativity, and openness (Yoshida, 2025). In a nutshell, both theoretical logic and empirical patterns suggest that in the ICU educational context, students’ extraordinary cognitive flexibility may function as a catalyst, facilitating their openness to different worldviews, such as by promoting perspective-shifting and tolerance for differences. Method

Participants

This qualitative research project used a Google Form survey (Appendix B) to ask six students from a freshman Research Writing class at ICU (male = 33%; female = 67%) about their worldviews, language experiences, and cognitive flexibility. 50% of the students (3/6) were 19 years old, and another 50% (3/6) were 20 years old. All students were unsure about their planned academic major but showed interest in a wide variety of subjects, including Cultural Anthropology, Philosophy, Psychology, Education, Economics, International Relations, Biology, and Sociology. 83.3% of the students (5/6) speak one of Japanese and English more fluently than the other, and one student speaks a third language; while 16.7% of the students (1/6) have the same fluency in Japanese and English. 67% students (4/6) did not perceive themselves as holding any particular worldview, while 33% students (2/6) claimed themselves as Shintoists. The survey answers (data) reported by the participants were summarized and analyzed using elements of the Grounded Theory approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). 

  

Data Analysis

As part for data analysis, composite scores were calculated for three variables based on survey responses: openness to different worldviews (scale: 0–10; derived from Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q7), language diversity (scale: 0–11; derived from D7, Q6, and Q8), and cognitive flexibility (scale: 0–10; derived from Q4 and Q5). First, the criteria of openness to different worldviews were defined as the sum of responses to Q1–Q3 and Q7. For Q1–Q3, responses were coded as No = 0, Maybe = 1, and Yes = 2. For Q7, levels of openness were coded on a four-level scale: Passive = 1, Slightly Active = 2, Active = 3, and Very Active = 4. Second, language diversity was calculated according to the sum of demographic question 7 (Numbers of Language ==  Scores; Native Fluency == 1, Not Fluent = 0.5), Q6 (English Use Rate <20% = 1, 20%-40%=2, 40%-60% = 3, 60%-80%= 4, >80% = 5), and Q8 (According to language diversity in talking serious topic, coding from 0-3). is the Sum of language number (1-3), English use rate in daily life (1-5), and language diversity in discussing serious topics (0-2). Third, the score of cognitive flexibility is the sum of responses to Q4 and Q5, each rated on a five-point scale, ultimately ranging from 0 to 10. The final result of coded data is shown in Table 1 (Appendix A). After coding, descriptive statistics , including mean values and standard deviation, were calculated for all variables (Figure 3, Appendix A). Furthermore, linear regression and pairwise correlations were conducted to explore the relations among variables (Figures 5-8, Appendix A). (427 words)

  

Results and Discussion

The research data collected resulted in three important findings, which are (1) a generally high level of openness to different worldview among participants, (2) a moderate level of language diversity that shows a robust positive association with openness, and (3) a generally high level of cognitive flexibility among students that is slightly positively related to openness but partially constrained by religious identification. 

First, as shown in Figures 1 and 2 (Appendix A), 100% of students (6/6) are interested in exploring different worldviews, and 50% (3/6) are willing to participate in events that introduce them. This indicates a uniformly high baseline interest in worldview exploration among the participants. Furthermore, responses of open-ended questions illustrate the diverse motivations underlying this openness: three students point out the practical necessity of engaging with diverse worldviews because understanding how others think helps them avoid being conservative, remain flexible, and broaden horizons; two students explicitly differentiate knowing and agreement, stating that exploring diverse worldviews does not necessarily mean endorsement, but can be intellectual engagement, such as applying Christian ethics in life without being a Christian; one participant showed simple curiosity without any particular practical purpose. Although one student was concerned about the validity of the event to introduce different worldviews, the overall pattern suggests an open-minded orientation toward different worldviews. Quantitatively, this pattern is also reflected in the composite openness score, which shows a generally high level of openness to different worldviews among all participants (Mean = 7.17/10, Standard Deviation = 0.75, Figure 3, Appendix A). One possible explanation for this trend lies in the participants’ diverse culture and societal backgrounds: all participants are current ICU ELA Stream 2 students, and most of them had overseas experiences or International High school backgrounds, which may have increased their exposure to multicultural environments, thereby contributing to their high openness to different worldviews.

Secondly, all students speak English and Japanese, and most of the students (83.3%, 5/6, Figure 4, Appendix A) use English in daily life around 20%-40% or more, indicating a generally moderate level of language diversity among the participants. This pattern is also shown by the statistical result of language diversity that is measured by the number of languages, proportion of second language usage in life, and in discussing controversial issues comprehensively (Mean = 5.67/11, Standard Deviation = 1.33, Figure 1, Appendix A). This relatively high level of language diversity may be partly attributed to the ELA stream placement mechanism, which places students with stronger English proficiency into stream 2.  Furthermore, variation within the group is informative: two participants scored markedly higher on language diversity — one through an additional language beyond English and Japanese, the other through a substantially higher rate of daily English use. Both also show relatively high openness scores, suggesting a positive correlation between language diversity and openness to different worldviews. Statistically, this observation is supported by both the regression and correlation analysis (Figures 6 & 8, Appendix A). A positive slope value (β = 0.434) was observed in the linear regression between language diversity and openness, and a strong positive correlation (r = 0.77) was found between the two variables. The positive relationship indicates that using English or other languages as a second language enables individuals to access information across linguistic boundaries, engage with various perspectives, and reduce prejudice, thereby increasing openness to different worldviews.

Last but not least, the majority of participants (4/6; 66.7%) have relatively high cognitive flexibility, scoring above 8 out of 10 (Table 1, Appendix A). This pattern may be partly explained by the educational atmosphere of International Christian University, which emphasizes critical thinking and reflective learning, potentially cultivating higher cognitive flexibility among students. Statistically, a moderate positive relationship, including regression and correlation, was observed between cognitive flexibility and openness to worldview (β = 0.412, r = 0.44; Figure 6 and Figure 8, Appendix A). Specifically, cognitive flexibility in this study was measured by participants’ willingness to adjust their mindset when encountering disagreement or difficult situations. In this way, higher cognitive flexibility scores may be associated with greater openness to different worldviews, as individuals who demonstrate flexibility in daily life may also be more open to engaging with different worldviews. However, religious identification appears to complicate this relationship. Regardless of the high cognitive flexibility among individuals, religious commitment may limit openness to adopting different worldviews, thereby reducing overall openness. This observation is supported by the regression and correlation analyses between religious identification and openness (Figures 7 & 8, Appendix A), which show a negative relation between the two values (β = -0.25, r = -0.17). Additionally, the correlation between religious and cognitive flexibility is slightly positive (r = 0.17) (Figure 8, Appendix A), suggesting that religious belief does not necessarily reduce cognitive flexibility itself, but may constrain openness to different worldviews. As a result, the moderate positive correlation between cognitive flexibility and openness is affected by religious identification. (809 words)

  

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study examined the influences of both language diversity and cognitive flexibility on ICU students’ openness to different worldviews, thereby bridging the research gap in understanding the factors behind students’ openness. Specifically, based on both qualitative and quantitative analysis, the results revealed that both language diversity and cognitive flexibility are positively correlated with students’ openness to different worldviews at different levels. These findings imply that learning or using second languages, cultivating critical thinking skills, and interacting with people from multicultural backgrounds may contribute to individuals’ high openness and tolerance for differences. In a large sense, this result can be used as a reference for leaders, teachers, or staff in educational institutions, since the positive effects of language diversity and cognitive flexibility on students’ openness clearly suggest significant elements in modern education to cultivate students’ openness in order to construct a harmonious society in the future.

However, this research is limited by the small sample size of six people and the low diversity of participants, as all of them are ICU ELA stream 2 students, which suggests that the result of this study is meaningful but not statistically significant, and also cannot be generally applied to a more general context. Further research is needed to investigate a larger sample size, more diverse participants, as well as a more systematic questionnaire to increase the validity of the research and offer a more comprehensive perspective for people in educational institutions.
