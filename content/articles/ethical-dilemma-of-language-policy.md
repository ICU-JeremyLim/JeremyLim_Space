Ethical dilemmas refer to situations where a difficult choice must be made between several options, and there is no completely right or wrong answer. They are not only a moral conflict that happens in the imaginary scene or the medical field, but also frequently occur in almost every corner of human society, including languages, a medium we use to convey information in daily life. Notably, a worldwide dilemma frequently occurs in multilingual areas: the domination of the majority language conflicts with the preservation of minority languages and the linguistic human rights (LHRs), which is a fundamental human right of any individual in this world to learn, use, and enjoy their languages in a secure environment (Skutunabb-Kangas and Philipson 17). In particular, the core of decision-making that directly affects linguistic justice in this dilemma is language policy. From the lens of ethical theories, a language policy rooted in Communitarian values, such as those found in China, tends to promote a unified national language, while a language policy with an underlying Libertarian view, such as that of Switzerland, respects the autonomy of speakers from diverse linguistic backgrounds. Since both Communitarianism and Libertarianism have merits and drawbacks in resolving the ethical dilemmas of language policy, a balanced, contextual, and just language policy — though it may not be a flawless solution — should be pursued in the future.

  

The ethical dilemma of language policy lies in striking a balance between national unity and linguistic human rights. To analyze the ethical dilemma of language policy, it is indispensable to realize that, instead of a natural result, the formation of the majority-minority social-linguistic structure and policy is “a historically, socially, politically constructed process” (May 259).  On the one hand, the primary purpose of a nation in propagating a common, national, and unified language is to improve communication efficiency by eliminating the barriers between different languages. However, under a unified language policy, minority language users have to give up their mother tongue, which is tightly connected to their culture, emotion, and identity, and to learn, listen, and speak a language that does not originally belong to them, which can be viewed as an invasion of human rights. On the other hand, implementing a policy that allows individuals who speak minority languages to use their native language in education, work, and daily life is beneficial for preserving minority languages and respecting their freedom of language use. Nevertheless, a significant drawback of this free and unrestricted language policy is that people who speak different languages are unable to understand each other, ultimately resulting in potential misunderstandings and conflicts between ethnic groups. Ultimately, multiple factors interact with one another, resulting in varied responses to the linguistic justice dilemma worldwide, much like different positions on the spectrum, each with its own specific ethical judgments and rationales.

  

Although a monolingual language policy enhances communication efficiency and national integration, it also marginalizes minority language speakers and undermines linguistic human rights. The communitarian perspective, an ethical value behind the monolingual language policy, values the orderliness of a society as an integral part of its integrity and prioritizes the common benefits over individual freedom, regarding each individual in the society as having a social responsibility to contribute to the community's prosperity (Campbell 242). James Tollefson, a linguistic policy expert, articulates that, based on this belief, a nation will implement a monolingual policy, including the centralist policy and the assimilation policy, that the majority group rationalizes the exclusion of other languages from public domains or encourages subordinate groups to adopt the mainstream language of the society to ensure the control of political and economic power (180). A typical case of a monolingual policy based on communitarian values is the promotion of the standard Chinese language policy (Tuipu) in China. China possesses a diverse range of languages, including spoken, written, and written systems, such as Hokkien and its romanized orthography, Tibetan and its script, as well as the Jingpo language and its written representation. In an extreme case, people in two nearby villages cannot understand each other’s language. This diversity is viewed by the Chinese government as an obstacle to national unity and development. The authority believes that Tuipu facilitates national economic, educational, and cultural development, thereby boosting societal progress; therefore, Tuipu is a necessity (Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China). Admittedly, Tuipu successfully demonstrates these advantages stated by the authority. In particular, Tuipu enables the majority of Chinese people to be equipped with a basic level of standard Mandarin through both education policy and social atmosphere that considers speaking standard Chinese as noble and civilized behavior, which empowers them to travel, relocate, and work in different cities in China, resulting in the fast and high quality development after the reformation and open policy since 1982. Nevertheless, the implementation of promoting the unified national language in China aligns with the Soviet model, which stressed collective interests and disregarded the linguistic human rights of minority language speakers (Zhou 323). For instance, in January 2021, the Legal Affairs Committee of the Chinese National People’s Congress (CNPC) claimed that the use of minority languages and scripts as the medium of instruction in local schools is unconstitutional (Liu). This decision violated the LHRs and facilitated the disappearance of minority languages in China. As a result of fostering the Tuipu policy, the human civilizational diversity, the intangible cultural heritage, and the unique identity of minority language speakers in China have been threatened, disrespected, and persecuted.

  

Similarly, although a multilingual language policy promotes linguistic equality and preserves cultural diversity, it has the drawback of being difficult to implement effectively in complex political and sociolinguistic contexts. The libertarian ideology, the ethical basis behind multilingual language policy, values individual freedom and autonomy, while strictly limiting the power of authorities over individuals (Campbell 244). This point of view leads to the formation of a multilingual language policy that “undermines the link between (typically one) language and nation-state” and respects the linguistic diversity and LHRs (Kraus and Grin 3). Several advantages of exerting a multicultural language policy are apparent. First of all, implementing a multicultural language policy helps reduce inequalities among people and prevent minority groups from marginalization, aligning with the 10th goal, which is to reduce inequity, of the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (Tollefson 3). In particular, such a language policy plays a crucial role in promoting the prosperity of local ethnic groups, as people who speak minority languages, including children, women, and local leaders, tend to understand information in their mother tongue more effectively than in other languages (SIL). Subsequently, they are able to comprehend instructions related to health, environmental protection, and development, which facilitates the improvement of maternal health, the combating of diseases, and ensures the sustainable development of local areas, ultimately benefiting the local community (SIL). Furthermore, a multilingual language policy is conducive to preserving the diversity of human civilization because it offers a secure and encouraging environment for people to creatively and freely use their language as a carrier of culture and identity. A case in point is Nanyin, an ancient folk music style of the Hokkien language recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage (UNESCO). When the Hokkien government implemented a tolerant language policy, Nanyin, an endangered art form, was revitalized, enriching the cultural diversity of the local community. However, the multilingual language policy is not without its flaws. Firstly, it can be a political compromise to prevent the nation from division, but it is not applicable in reality. For example, both English and French are official languages of Canada. Nevertheless, in a legal case in 1986, the Canadian Supreme Court ruled that “summons did not need to be bilingual”, and “French-speaking citizens did not have the right to be understood by the courts in their official language.”  (Cardinal and Leger 27) In other words, the implementation of multilingualism in Canada's legal institutions falls short of ensuring the equal use of both official languages, suggesting that symbolic recognition at a superficial level of linguistic equity does not guarantee practical implementation. From this case, we can draw the conclusion that if the LHRs of using the second-largest official language of Canada in a public institution cannot be ensured, much less other non-official minority languages, then the multilingual language policy based on political compromise is an empty promise. Secondly, multilingualism in favor of language diversity is unlikely to gain significant support in the near future due to the dominant position of English in international society. Examining the case of EU multiculturalism policy, although the EU-level laws acknowledge the quality of the 24 languages of the member states, the sociolinguistic current situation in Europe shows that people who are well-educated or actively in international communication are more fluent in English than their mother tongue and value communication efficiency over the preservation of linguistic diversity (Barbier 345). Thus, the fatal weakness of multilingualism is its superficial formalization, with challenges in applying it to the complicated real world.

  

Given the limitations of both monolingual and multilingual language policies, a balanced approach should promote a unified national language as a bridge for nationwide communication, ensuring efficiency and security, while simultaneously safeguarding minority languages and linguistic human rights in the policy-making process. A notable example is Singapore's language policy. Given Singapore's unique national situation, which lacks natural resources and heavily relies on industry, international business, and the intelligence of its people from diverse ethnic backgrounds, a language policy that respects diverse languages and ensures national unity, and fosters communication efficiency is an indispensable requirement (Wee and Bokhorst-Heng 164). From 1963, the Singapore government initiated a relatively balanced, contextualized, and still impactful language policy: Mandating English as the only nationwide common language and other languages spoken by the majority of Singaporeans, such as Mandarin, Malay, and Tamil, as official languages;  all students are required to learn English and a one of other official language as their mother tongue language (Lee). As a result of implementing this policy over several generations, English language proficiency among Singaporeans enables people to access scientific and technological knowledge, empowers the nation to attract foreign investment, and alleviates the barrier between heterogeneous ethnic groups, thereby boosting the country's overall economic development. Simultaneously, emphasizing the values of mother languages preserves the diversity of Singapore society, respects the unique identity of each ethnic group, and lays a solid foundation for the construction of a harmonious society (Lee). Although Singapore provides a near-perfect example for a balanced and contextualized language policy, the policy still has its deficiencies. First, the list of mother languages excludes minority languages, such as Hokkien and Hakka, in order to ensure the efficiency of the educational system. This decision is based on the ideology of consequentialism, which holds that as long as the happiness, encompassing LHRs, educational quality, and communication efficiency, of the majority of Singaporean people is preserved, it is justifiable to sacrifice the preservation of minority languages. Ultimately, this policy exacerbates the situation of minority languages and accelerates their disappearance, jeopardizing the diversity of human civilization. Additionally, Singapore’s language policy is not a universally applicable policy, but rather a limited, regional fine-tune policy, as other countries in the world have larger territories, more diverse populations and languages, and more complex national situations, such as immigration from multiple regions and conflicts between ethnical groups, compared to Singapore. Therefore, the process of creating a language policy is similar to navigating a trolley problem-like ethical dilemma, as no choice is flawless; however, a relatively optimal and rational decision should be made according to the socioeconomic situation of a nation and the ideology of the policymakers.

  

In conclusion, the core of the ethical dilemma of language policy is striking a balance between unity and diversity. While monolingual policies, rooted in communitarian approaches, aim to promote national unity and efficiency, and multilingualism, based on libertarianism, preserves individual freedom and linguistic diversity, neither can achieve linguistic justice on its own. However, a balanced, contextualized, and optimized policy, such as the Singaporean model, despite its minor drawbacks, offers a more realistic, applicable, and ethical solution. In a large sense, the moral foundation of language policy parallels some of the bioethical principles: autonomy, justice, and balance (Campbell 245-247). These shared principles reveal the essentials of ethical dilemmas that require constant negotiation to strike a balance between the collective good and individual rights in the pursuit of an optimal solution.
