According to a famous traditional Chinese saying, “All other pursuits are inferior; only studying is truly noble.” This thought originated in imperial China, where the only legitimate way to secure an official position was to study and pass the Keju, the imperial examination system in ancient China. Although the Keju era ended more than one hundred years ago, this saying has continuously been echoed in school, family, and broader Chinese society. One fundamental reason is the structural similarity between cramming education in modern China and the Keju system in imperial China: both are life-determining, high-stakes examinations conducted under high pressure and expectation (Hafsteinsson 25-28). Many Chinese people believe that this system is equal since it enables students from economically disadvantaged families to change their lives by achieving high scores in the national examination  (“Wangyi Wangzhan”). However, they underestimate the problems of cramming education, such as impeding critical thinking and causing anxiety. In this essay, I will explore how the restriction of holistic development and the rise of mental health issues among students are problems of cramming education in modern China; furthermore, I will discuss how the top-down reform from the policy level and the bottom-up reform inside the education system can be effective solutions.

The first problem with cramming education in China is that exam-oriented education restricts the holistic development of students. The holistic development covers not only intellectual growth, but also students’ emotional, social, physical, artistic, moral, and spiritual development, which are expected to be nurtured through a balanced curriculum and integrated teaching methods (Gordon 1). However, the cramming culture overemphasizes the importance of scores in national exams while overlooking the importance of well-rounded development, leading to numerous negative results. For example, many teachers of the core subjects, such as Mathematics, English, and Science, occupy the class time of non-core subjects, including art, music, and physical exercise. These teacher's purpose is to improve the average score of their subject, thereby resulting in better rewards and promotion for themselves (“Xinhua Wang”). In this way, students have little opportunity to explore the aesthetic experience, strengthen their bodies and broaden the scope of interests  (“Xinhua Wang”). Furthermore, pursuing high scores in the cramming educational system damages students’ creativity and curiosity (M. Liu 1-4). Instead of actively asking questions, seeking intellectual understanding, or expressing their opinions independently, students are often trained to follow the fixed answer, ultimately achieving higher scores at the expense of their independent thinking and intrinsic interest toward knowledge (M. Liu 1-4). Thus, students' well-rounded development, such as aesthetic value, physical skills, and critical thinking, is impeded by the cramming education due to the score-centred doctrine. 

Another problem caused by cramming education is mental issues, such as anxiety, loneliness, and depression. Firstly, the highly competitive educational system in schools provokes anxiety among students (Fu 29-30). It is widely known in China that schools tend to publish grade rankings to motivate students to focus on improving their scores. Schools praise top students who achieved high grades or made progress, while students who perform poorly in examinations will be regarded as bad students. This ranking system tightly attached students’ self-esteem, honor, and confidence to their grades, leading to a sense of insecurity and anxiety because top students fear that they may not able to perform as well as before and tail students worry about they will be mocked by their peers and teachers due to their low scores (“Anhui Jianzhu Daxue”). Particularly, in the final grade of junior high school or high school, frequent examinations will be held, which exacerbate the mental health of students and cause anxiety disorders (Cheng 2). An additional harm in the mental aspect caused by cramming education is the competition with peers. Thus, students tend to regard their classmates as rivals instead of friends, which can lead to difficulty collaborating with peers and establishing a trustworthy friendship with others and, in turn, to a sense of loneliness (Zhou 9). A further negative influence on mental health from cramming culture is that it forces students to prioritize their learning for examinations, so the most legitimate pursuit for students is studying, and other activities tend to be regarded as distracting and even guilty (Fu 25). As a result, studying time replaces students’ social and relaxing time, which is the main reason for depression among students (Fu 25). Therefore, cramming education negatively impacts students’ mental health directly or indirectly in multiple ways.

Macroscopically, top-down reform at the policy level is essential in solving the overcompetition issue in cramming education. According to The State Council of the People’s Republic of China, 13.42 million students attended the national examination, the Gaokao, in China for college application in 2024. While the participants are more than the total population of Greece, fewer than 5% of them could enter a top university (The State Council of the People’s Republic of China). Thus, if there were diverse paths to enter college, the competition among students would be alleviated, therefore addressing the issues of mental health and holistic development. From my perspective, the two key approaches are diversifying college admission pathways and introducing the German tripartite educational system. The first approach is offering students alternative pathways for university admission that take students’ comprehensive ability into consideration in the evaluation. For example, apart from the general entrance examination, the majority of top universities in Japan conduct the admission office entrance examination (AO) to enroll diverse students who may not be skilled in achieving high scores in standardized tests but excel at critical thinking (“Kokosei Shinbun”). In the same way, China can imitate this pattern to diversify the path for students to enter college, expecting to result in a reduction of pressure and competition (H. Liu 16-17). Another approach is to take inspiration from the German tripartite educational system for the education system in China. After primary school, based on students’ academic performance and career orientation, the tripartite system sorts students into three categories, which are Gymnasium, Realschule, and Hauptschule (“Zhongguo Jiaoyu Xinxihua Wang”). Gymnasium prepares students for university, Realschule balances intermediate academic and practical skills, and Hauptschule offers vocational training—together enabling personalized learning and easing academic competition as well as the mental stress of students (“Zhongguo Jiaoyu Xinxihua Wang”). If China could incorporate this pattern into its education system, it would simultaneously alleviate the competition in the college entrance exam and activate the vocational education system (“Zhongguo Jiaoyu Xinxihua Wang”). Although both the AO entrance examination and tripartite educational system have limitations, the benefits of introducing them adaptively to the Chinese cramming educational system outweigh their disadvantages (H. Liu 16-17 & “Zhongguo Jiaoyu Xinxihua Wang”). Therefore, by diversifying the path of entrance examination and sorting students into different academic paths, the Chinese education system could be less competitive and better balanced.

Microscopically, bottom-up reforms inside the Chinese cramming education system by empowering the autonomy of teachers are indispensable. Currently, rather than direct implementers of curriculum and instruction in classes, teachers are more like "teaching machines" of cramming education (Cheng 9). Due to their heightened accountability for students' scores from both the school system, whose reputation is highly related to students’ scores, and anxious parents, who have high expectations for teachers to raise their children’s scores, teachers have to rigidly follow outdated textbooks and repetitively hold mock exams (Cheng 9). This rigid pattern contributes to key problems in cramming education,  particularly the restriction of holistic development. To tackle this issue, grassroots-level reforms are expected to be implemented by the fundamental actors of the educational system, including schools and parents, giving teachers more autonomy to optimize their pedagogy  (Cheng 10). Specifically, in Chinese society, it is widely observed that teachers who suffered from cramming education are often eager to reform the stressful and ineffective educational environment when allowed sufficient freedom in teaching (Yang 125). With teaching autonomy, teachers are able to explore diverse and suitable teaching strategies from the bottom side of the educational system, such as game-based learning (GBL) and group discussion (Hung 1-2). Such bottom-up initiatives are not driven by centralized policy but by frontline teachers who understand the problems in their educational field well and are willing to fulfill the students’ needs (Hung 1-2). For instance, through combining GBL and the flipped classroom into an English class, students are motivated to actively expand vocabulary, collaborate with peers, and develop effective learning strategies, resulting in the improvement of independent thinking and teamwork competence (Hung 3-5).  An additional benefit of empowering teachers’ autonomy is that teachers can take students’ motivation, critical thinking, and other non-academic dimensions into consideration in evaluation (X.Liu). The merit of the diversification of assessment is to encourage students to focus on the quality of learning and the well-roundedness of their growth, reducing the pressure brought by grade competition (X.Liu). Therefore, enhancing teacher autonomy at the grassroots level helps tackle key issues of cramming education by promoting pedagogical innovation and more holistic evaluation.

In conclusion, cramming education in China originated from the ancient era and has had a significant impact on Chinese society. It hinders the holistic development of students because of the marginalization of non-core subjects and the prioritization of grades. Furthermore, cramming education in China causes mental health issues among students, such as anxiety, due to the fierce competition and excessive learning time. To solve these problems, both top-down policies, such as diversifying the college entrance path, and bottom-up solutions, like empowering teachers’ autonomy, are required. The limitation of this essay is that neither type of solution can specifically deal with mental issues using a psychological approach because there is no known research about it. In the future, Chinese education could be improved by raising awareness of those issues among policymakers, teachers, and students, and continuing to reform the education system at both macroscopic and microscopic levels.
